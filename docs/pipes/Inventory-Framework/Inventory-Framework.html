AI Act for Banking - A Use-Case Inventory Framework for Structured Compliance

AI Governance
Banking Regulation
EU AI Act
Model Risk
Author

Ashwin Bijur

Published

February 20, 2026

Executive Summary

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework under which obligations apply to identifiable AI systems according to their classification (e.g., high-risk, limited-risk). Requirements relating to documentation, governance, monitoring, and human oversight are therefore triggered at the system level rather than at the level of general enterprise policy.

For banks, this structure implies the need to:

  • Identify AI systems in scope
  • Determine their risk classification
  • Map applicable regulatory requirements
  • Demonstrate implemented controls

An enterprise-wide AI use-case inventory provides the structural basis for performing this analysis consistently across business units and model types.

This article outlines a framework tailored to banking environments where model risk management, operational risk, outsourcing oversight, and data governance functions already exist, but where alignment to system-level AI Act obligations requires additional integration and documentation discipline.

1. Why Inventory Precedes Compliance Structuring

The political agreement reached in December 2023 on the EU Artificial Intelligence Act confirms a risk-based framework distinguishing:

  • Prohibited AI practices
  • High-risk AI systems
  • Limited-risk systems
  • Minimal-risk systems[^1]

Obligations are triggered at the AI system level.

Most banks maintain:

  • Model inventories
  • Data governance registers
  • Outsourcing risk registers
  • ICT risk frameworks

However, these are typically structured around models, vendors, or data assets rather than around AI systems as defined in regulatory scope.

A cross-enterprise AI system inventory enables:

  • Consistent risk classification
  • Traceability to business processes
  • Identification of high-risk systems
  • Structured documentation readiness

Without a system-level inventory, classification decisions may vary across departments, and evidence assembly becomes fragmented.

1.1 Structural Failure Modes in AI Act Readiness

An inventory-led approach reduces structural risk, but several predictable failure modes should be anticipated and governed explicitly.

1.1.1 Misclassification Risk and Regulatory Exposure

Risk classification determines the applicable obligations. Misclassification can occur due to:

  • Over-reliance on business interpretation of “intended purpose”

  • Underestimation of downstream impact on individuals

  • Treating advisory systems as low-impact without analysing practical decision weight

Regulatory exposure arises where:

  • A system should have been classified as high-risk but was not

  • Documentation obligations were therefore not fulfilled

  • Conformity assessment requirements were not triggered

Classification decisions should therefore be independently reviewed and formally recorded.

1.1.2 Inventory Drift Over Time

AI system inventories are not static artefacts.

Drift occurs when:

  • Systems evolve through incremental enhancements

  • LLM integrations are added to previously non-AI workflows

  • Decision influence increases without reclassification

  • Legacy systems are repurposed

Without periodic reclassification review, inventory accuracy degrades and compliance posture becomes misaligned with operational reality.

Recommended control:

  • Annual classification review

  • Mandatory reassessment upon material system change

  • Trigger-based reassessment for scope expansion

1.1.3 Business Unit Under-Reporting

Business functions may under-report AI usage due to:

  • Ambiguity around what constitutes an AI system

  • Perceived regulatory burden

  • Pilot-stage deployments not considered “in production”

  • Embedded AI within vendor SaaS platforms

This creates regulatory blind spots.

Mitigation mechanisms:

  • Clear AI system definition guidance

  • Attestation requirements by business unit heads

  • Integration of AI declaration into procurement and change processes

  • Internal audit sampling of unregistered AI usage

1.1.4 GPAI Vendor Dependency and Opacity

Foundation models and General Purpose AI systems introduce additional complexity.

Opacity risks include:

  • Limited visibility into training data sources

  • Unclear allocation of provider versus deployer obligations

  • Incomplete technical documentation access

  • Limited assurance on post-market monitoring by vendors

Where high-risk use cases rely on external General Purpose AI (GPAI) systems, banks must document:

  • Provider obligation mapping

  • Contractual allocation of responsibilities

  • Monitoring of upstream model changes

  • Contingency planning for vendor compliance failure

Vendor dependency should be incorporated into outsourcing and third-party risk frameworks.

2. Banking AI Use-Case Taxonomy for Classification

A functional taxonomy aligned to banking workflows supports consistent regulatory assessment.

2.1 Customer-Facing AI

Examples: - Chatbots and LLM-driven assistants
- Robo-advisory tools
- Automated onboarding systems

Assessment consideration: Does the system materially affect access to financial services or rights?

Annex III of the provisional text classifies AI systems evaluating access to essential services — including credit — as high-risk[^2].

2.2 Credit & Risk Decisioning

Examples: - Retail credit scoring
- SME risk rating systems
- Automated limit allocation

These systems fall explicitly within high-risk categories under Annex III.

Banks commonly apply model validation, performance monitoring, and backtesting. The AI Act extends this framework to include:

  • Formalized technical documentation
  • Data governance specifications
  • Defined human oversight design
  • Post-market monitoring obligations

2.3 Fraud & AML Systems

Examples: - Transaction monitoring ML
- Fraud detection models
- Sanctions screening NLP

Risk classification depends on:

  • Degree of automation
  • Decision impact on individuals
  • Level of human intervention

Documentation should clearly distinguish between advisory systems and automated enforcement mechanisms.

2.4 Internal Operations & Generative AI

Examples: - Intelligent document processing
- GenAI summarization tools
- Internal copilots

These systems may fall into limited-risk or minimal-risk categories depending on impact. Classification must be documented with reference to intended purpose and system design.

The Act introduces specific provisions for General Purpose AI (GPAI) models and foundation models integrated downstream[^3]. Deployers must assess the allocation of obligations between providers and institutional users.

3. Control Mapping at System Level

Following classification, controls should be mapped systematically to each AI system.

3.1 Data Governance

High-risk systems must meet data governance requirements addressing relevance, representativeness, and bias mitigation[^4].

Banking alignment typically involves:

  • Data quality frameworks
  • Lineage documentation
  • Bias testing protocols

3.2 Technical Documentation

High-risk systems require documentation sufficient to demonstrate regulatory conformity.

Existing model documentation practices can be expanded to include:

  • System-level scope definitions
  • Intended purpose statements
  • Risk classification justification

3.3 Monitoring & Post-Market Surveillance

The Act introduces post-market monitoring obligations.

Operational alignment includes:

  • Performance dashboards
  • Drift detection
  • Incident logging
  • Periodic review mechanisms

Monitoring processes must be demonstrable and auditable.

3.4 Human Oversight

Human oversight mechanisms must be designed into system architecture.

Supervisory examination will focus on:

  • Override authority
  • Escalation pathways
  • Accountability assignment
  • Reviewer qualification

Oversight must be operationally meaningful.

3.5 Transparency & Explainability

Transparency obligations apply where individuals interact with AI systems.

For high-risk systems, explanation capability becomes critical, particularly in credit and eligibility contexts.

3.6 Incident Reporting

Serious incidents involving high-risk AI systems must be documented and reported under defined procedures.

Banks should incorporate AI-specific categorization within existing operational risk frameworks.

4. Intersection with Existing Banking Supervision

There is alignment with:

  • EBA Guidelines on Loan Origination and Monitoring
  • EBA Guidelines on ICT and Security Risk Management
  • ECB supervisory expectations on model risk

However, AI Act compliance differs structurally:

Banking Supervision Focus AI Act Focus
Model-level governance System-level conformity
Prudential risk Fundamental rights impact
Internal validation Regulatory technical documentation
Risk appetite Formal risk classification

Existing governance structures provide foundational capabilities but do not automatically satisfy AI Act conformity requirements.

5. Evidence Pack Structuring

Regulatory readiness requires system-centric evidence.

A high-risk AI evidence package should include:

  1. System description and intended purpose
  2. Risk classification rationale
  3. Data governance documentation
  4. Validation and testing artefacts
  5. Monitoring logs and performance evidence
  6. Human oversight documentation
  7. Change management history
  8. Vendor documentation where applicable

Documentation should be structured per system to enable efficient supervisory review.

6. Explicit Operating Model for AI System Governance

An inventory-based framework requires clear role allocation across lines of defence.

The following structure provides analytical clarity and supervisory defensibility.

6.1 First Line of Defence — System Owner

Role: Business owner of the AI system.

Responsibilities:

  • Declare system inclusion in AI inventory

  • Document intended purpose

  • Initiate risk classification proposal

  • Ensure operational monitoring

  • Maintain system-level documentation

  • Trigger reassessment upon material change

Accountability rests with the business owner for system accuracy and completeness.

6.2 Second Line of Defence — Risk & Classification Validator

Role: Independent validation function (e.g., Model Risk, Enterprise Risk).

Responsibilities:

  • Review risk classification determination

  • Validate documentation completeness

  • Assess alignment with Annex III criteria

  • Challenge advisory vs determinative positioning

  • Confirm human oversight adequacy

Classification should not be self-certified by the business function.

6.3 Compliance Function — Regulatory Conformity Sign-Off

Role: Regulatory interpretation and conformity oversight.

Responsibilities:

  • Confirm applicable AI Act obligations

  • Validate documentation against regulatory requirements

  • Review transparency obligations

  • Approve high-risk system conformity positioning

Formal sign-off should be recorded for high-risk systems prior to deployment or material modification.

6.4 Escalation Path

Clear escalation is required where:

  • Classification disputes arise

  • Significant incidents occur

  • Vendor compliance risk is identified

  • Documentation gaps persist

Recommended escalation path:

  1. Business Owner

  2. Risk Validation Committee

  3. AI Governance Committee

  4. Executive Risk Committee

Escalation thresholds should be predefined and documented.

6.5 Board and Executive Reporting

AI governance requires structured reporting at board level.

Recommended minimum reporting cadence:

  • Semi-annual AI system inventory summary

  • Quarterly high-risk system monitoring overview

  • Immediate escalation for serious incidents

Board reporting should include:

  • Total AI systems by classification tier

  • Number of high-risk systems

  • Documentation completeness status

  • Incident summary

  • Vendor dependency exposure

Board-level visibility ensures alignment with institutional risk appetite.

7. Phased Implementation Approach

Phase 1 (0–6 months)

  • Establish enterprise AI system register
  • Conduct initial risk classification
  • Assign accountable owners

Phase 2 (6–12 months)

  • Map regulatory requirements to high-risk systems
  • Close documentation gaps
  • Standardize technical documentation templates

Phase 3 (12–18 months)

  • Integrate monitoring and reporting workflows
  • Conduct internal readiness assessments
  • Validate oversight mechanisms

Conclusion

The EU AI Act introduces system-level obligations structured around risk classification and demonstrable control implementation.

For banks, compliance readiness depends on the institution’s ability to:

  • Maintain an accurate AI system inventory

  • Apply consistent risk classification criteria

  • Map regulatory requirements to implemented controls

  • Evidence monitoring and oversight mechanisms

  • Govern vendor dependencies transparently

Failure modes such as misclassification, inventory drift, under-reporting, and GPAI opacity introduce structural compliance risk if not addressed explicitly.

An inventory-led governance model, supported by clear role allocation and board visibility, provides a scalable mechanism for aligning existing banking control environments with AI Act conformity requirements.

AI Act readiness therefore becomes a question of operational governance integration rather than policy formulation.
